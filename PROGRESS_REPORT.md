## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Overview

Sequence models must often maintain a *state*: a structured quantity whose
correct value at step *t* depends on every symbol seen before it. Transformers
solve this by re-reading the whole history at every step, which costs time
quadratic in sequence length. Linear recurrent models — linear attention,
state-space models, and the delta-rule family — instead carry a fixed-size state
forward, giving cost linear in length, and have historically been weaker at
exactly this kind of state tracking.

**DeltaProduct** (Siems et al., NeurIPS 2025) narrows that gap. At each step it
builds the state-transition matrix as a product of `n_h` generalized Householder
factors. More factors give a richer set of reachable transitions and cost
proportionally more computation. The design decision this project takes issue
with is that **`n_h` is a single global hyperparameter**, fixed by hand before
training and applied identically to every token in every sequence.

This project makes that count **per-token and learned**. A small monotone halting
gate reads each token and decides how many factors it actually receives; a budget
penalty pushes the model to be frugal; nothing supervises the allocation. The
work has two halves — an algebraic half that establishes what the *correct*
allocation is, and an engineering half that tries to learn it — and this report
separates them everywhere, because the first is finished and the second is not.

### 1.2 Motivation

The obvious motivation is efficiency: if most tokens need one factor and a few
need four, paying four everywhere wastes most of the computation. That argument
is real but, in this project, **not yet earned** — no wall-clock saving has been
measured, for reasons set out in §3.5, and it is not relied on anywhere in this
report.

The motivation that *is* earned is stronger and came out of the work rather than
preceding it. The project's principal result (§3.3) proves that the number of
factors a token requires is **not a property of that token**. It is determined by
the algebraic closure of the entire input alphabet. The consequence is concrete
and counter-intuitive: adding a single transposition — the cheapest symbol that
exists — to an alphabet of 5-cycles doubles the required order of every 5-cycle,
from two factors to four, because it changes which group the alphabet generates
and therefore which faithful representations are available.

This means `n_h` is **a hyperparameter nobody can set correctly**. Choosing it
requires knowing the minimum faithful representation dimension of the group
generated by the data, which is not something a practitioner can read off a
dataset. In practice one over-provisions and wastes computation, or
under-provisions and fails silently. A layer that infers its own order removes a
knob that cannot be turned by hand. That argument holds today and needs no
speedup to hold.

A secondary motivation emerged from the experimental record itself (§4.6):
training outcomes on the harder configurations are **bistable**, not noisy around
a mean, which makes single-run measurements of them uninformative. Recognising
this withdrew two claims from the project record and is treated here as a result
in its own right rather than as an inconvenience.

### 1.3 What this report covers

Sections 2 and 3 give the literature position and the proposed method, including
the two proved bounds the design is derived from. Section 4 reports every
experiment run to date under the labelling scheme above. Section 5 states what is
established, what is not, and the pre-registered fallbacks if the remaining work
fails.

## 2. Literature Review

### 2.1 Background and Related Work

#### 2.1.1 Linear recurrent models and state tracking

Linear recurrent architectures — linear attention, state-space models, and the
delta-rule family — achieve inference cost linear in sequence length, against the
quadratic cost of softmax attention. Their historical weakness is *state
tracking*: maintaining and updating a structured hidden state whose correct value
at step *t* depends on all *t* preceding symbols. Permutation-group tracking is
the standard probe for this capability because it admits no shortcut, is exactly
solvable on paper, and has a known difficulty parameter.

#### 2.1.2 The base method

**DeltaProduct** (Siems, Carstensen, Zela, Hutter, Pontil, Grazzi; NeurIPS 2025,
arXiv:2502.10297) constructs each recurrent state-transition matrix as a product
of `n_h` generalized Householder factors,

```
A_t = Π_{i=1..n_h} ( I − β_i k_i k_iᵀ ),     ‖k_i‖ = 1,  β_i ∈ [0, 2]
```

Increasing `n_h` increases the expressivity of the reachable transition set and
increases computation proportionally. **`n_h` is a single global hyperparameter,
fixed by hand before training and applied identically to every token.** The
paper's own future-work section identifies per-token adaptive order as an open
direction and cites Adaptive Computation Time.

#### 2.1.3 Adaptive computation

**Adaptive Computation Time** (Graves, 2016) and **PonderNet** (Banino, Balaguer,
Blundell, 2021) supply the standard monotone halting mechanism for letting a
model choose how much computation to spend per input, trained with a penalty on
expected computation. These are used off the shelf in this project and are not
claimed as a contribution.

#### 2.1.4 Expressivity of linear recurrent models

**Shakerinava, Khavari, Ravanbakhsh & Chandar** (ICLR 2026) study the
expressivity of input-dependent complex-valued diagonal SSMs on state-tracking
tasks, and report that multi-layer models **often fail to learn state tracking
for non-Abelian groups** — a gap between representational capacity and
optimisation. This project observes the same phenomenon for Householder products
(§4.6).

#### 2.1.5 Classical result used

**Cartan–Dieudonné** gives the reflection count for an orthogonal transformation:
every element of O(n) is a composition of at most *n* reflections, and every
**non-identity** rotation in SO(3) is a composition of exactly two. This supplies
the lower-bound machinery in §3.2. The step this project takes beyond it is the
*representation-dependence of the minimum* (§3.3), which does not appear in the
literature above.

### 2.2 Outcome of the Literature Review

The literature fixes `n_h` globally and treats the required expressivity as a
per-token property. Two gaps follow from reading it against this project's own
algebraic work:

**Gap 1 — the cost model is wrong.** The assumption that a token's required order
is a property of that token is contradicted by §3.3, which proves the requirement
is a property of the closure of the entire alphabet. This is not a refinement of
DeltaProduct's analysis; it contradicts the cost accounting implicit in it, and
it does not appear in any of the works above.

**Gap 2 — the hyperparameter is unsettable.** Because the correct `n_h` depends
on the minimum faithful representation dimension of the generated group, it
cannot be determined from inspection of the data. Global fixing is therefore not
a simplification but a forced over- or under-provisioning.

**What the field already supplies.** The halting mechanism itself is solved work
(§2.1.3) and is used unmodified. The gap this project addresses is not "how does
a model halt" but "what is the correct amount of computation, and can it be
inferred without supervision".

> **Survey gap, stated honestly.** The references above are the works this
> project has engaged with in depth. A broader survey of the linear-attention and
> SSM landscape (Mamba/S4 lineage, gated linear attention, hybrid architectures)
> has not yet been carried out and is scheduled before the final report (§5.2).

### 2.3 Problem Statement

> In the DeltaProduct family of linear recurrent models, the number of
> Householder factors per token, `n_h`, is a global hyperparameter applied
> uniformly to every input symbol. The expressivity actually required varies
> across symbols, so a fixed-order model must provision for the worst case and
> pay that cost everywhere. Furthermore, the required value cannot be determined
> from local properties of the tokens. The problem is to make the order
> **per-token, learned, and cheaper to execute**, without loss of accuracy.

## 3. Proposed Methodology

### 3.1 Research methodology: two layers of evidence

The project deliberately separates two kinds of evidence, and every claim in §4
is attributed to one of them.

**The algebraic layer.** Questions about what the architecture *can represent*
are answered by explicit construction in float64 on CPU, never by training a
model and observing what it reaches. This was adopted after an early error in
which an optimiser collapsed to `A = I` and the result was misread as
"unreachable". Constructions are verified elementwise against known targets, and
no claim in §4.2–4.3 depends on an optimiser converging.

**The empirical layer.** Questions about what the architecture *learns* are
answered by GPU training runs on generated state-tracking data, reported with the
number of runs stated.

The two are never mixed. A representability question answered by a training run
would be evidence about the optimiser, not about the architecture.

### 3.2 Theoretical foundation I — the two hard bounds (PROVED)

**Bound 1 — rank.** Each Householder factor differs from the identity by a
rank-1 matrix, so `rank(A − I) ≤ K` for a product of K factors. For a permutation
matrix, `rank(P − I) = n − cycles(P) = ℓ(P)`. Therefore `K ≥ ℓ` is forced, for
every β, free or pinned. Verified over d ∈ {5, 8, 16}, K ∈ 0..6.

**Bound 2 — parity.** With β pinned at 2, `det(A) = (−1)^K` while
`det(P) = (−1)^ℓ`, so only `K ≡ ℓ (mod 2)` is reachable. Verified by explicit
construction on **all 120 elements of S₅ at every K**, worst reconstruction error
**1.17 × 10⁻¹⁵**.

These two bounds are stated before the mechanism because the second of them
**dictates** a design decision that would otherwise look arbitrary (§3.4).

### 3.3 Theoretical foundation II — the corrected cost model (PROVED — the project's principal result)

The assumption implicit in the literature is that the matrix the layer must apply
is the *permutation matrix of the token*. It is not. The layer may apply ρ(g) for
**any faithful representation ρ of the group generated by the whole alphabet**,
and ρ is the layer's choice. Hence:

> **D(g) = min over faithful representations ρ of ⟨alphabet⟩ of rank(ρ(g) − I)**

A 5-cycle costs **4 factors inside S₅** and **2 inside A₅**, because A₅ has a
3-dimensional faithful representation — it is the rotation group of the
icosahedron — and S₅ has none. In SO(3) every non-identity rotation fixes only
its axis, so `rank(R − I) = 2`, and by Cartan–Dieudonné factors into exactly two
reflections.

Verified in `work/exp_minimal_order.py` by constructing the group from geometry:
60 distinct rotations, closure error 1.4e-16, element-order profile
{1:1, 2:15, 3:20, 5:24} matching A₅, the isomorphism exhibited explicitly via the
action on the five inscribed octahedra, every non-identity element of rank 2, and
two-reflection factorisation to **1.31 × 10⁻¹⁵**.

**The consequence, which is the counter-intuitive part:** the cost of a token is
a property of the *whole alphabet*. Adding a single transposition — the cheapest
symbol that exists — to an alphabet of 5-cycles closes it over S₅ instead of A₅,
destroys the 3-dimensional representation, and **doubles the cost of every
5-cycle from 2 to 4.** An easy symbol makes the task harder. No per-token
difficulty model can produce this.

**Where the method cannot help, stated in advance.** Where D is constant across
the alphabet — A₄ and A₅ — adaptive order offers exactly nothing. The ability to
predict this before running any experiment is a stronger position than a claim
that the method always helps.

### 3.4 The proposed mechanism

A small **monotone halting gate** reads each token and emits a per-token count
`n_t ≤ n_h`; factors beyond `n_t` are disabled. Monotonicity means the gate
cannot skip a factor and resume, so the count is a single number. A **budget
loss** penalises the mean `n_t`, pushing the model to be frugal with no
supervision on the allocation itself.

**A design constraint forced by theory.** The gate must *scale a learnable β*,
not mask a fixed one. This is not a stylistic choice — §3.2, Bound 2 proves that
with β pinned at 2, padding a solution of length ℓ up to K factors is only
possible when `K ≡ ℓ (mod 2)`, because the only identity constructible from
pinned reflections is a *pair*. A learnable β can construct an identity from a
*single* factor at β = 0, which makes every K ≥ ℓ reachable. The implementation
multiplies the gate into β after the `2·sigmoid(·)` nonlinearity, satisfying
this.

**Training protocol.** Gates are soft while training, for smooth gradients, and
**hard at evaluation**, so every reported cost is an integer count of factors
rather than a sum of sigmoids. The gate initialises open, so at step 0 the model
is exactly fixed-order DeltaProduct — the configuration known to converge — and
the budget penalty is ramped in after a warm-up rather than applied from step 1,
so the gate cannot close before the task is learned. A γ = 0 arm, in which the
gate is installed but never penalised, is the control for the gate's mere
presence.

### 3.5 Execution paths A and B

- **Path A (implemented).** All `n_h` factors are computed; disabled ones are
  multiplied by zero. Numerically correct, and **yields no wall-clock saving.**
- **Path B (NOT STARTED).** Ragged expansion that genuinely skips disabled
  factors. This is what would convert the required-work saving into measured time.

Every cost figure in this report is Path A, and therefore **notional**. This is
the single largest gap between what the project claims and what it has
demonstrated, and it is stated here rather than left for a reader to find.

### 3.6 Experimental setup

**Hardware.** NVIDIA RTX 4050 Laptop GPU, 6 GB VRAM, WSL2 on Windows.

**Software.** PyTorch with Triton kernels; `flash-linear-attention` vendored and
patched, with original files retained as `.orig` alongside. All modifications to
upstream files are marked `PATCH(adaptive-order)` in-line.

**Model.** 1 layer, 12 heads × 32 head-dim, hidden 128, bf16.

**Data.** Sequences of length k = 128 over a chosen alphabet of permutations;
the target at each position is the cumulative product of all preceding symbols.
100 000 training sequences, 2 000 held-out test sequences unless stated.

**Optimisation.** AdamW, learning rate 1e-3 constant, batch 128, 20 000 steps
unless stated.

**Reporting.** Token accuracy is the fraction of positions correct. Sequence
accuracy requires every position in a sequence to be correct and is far harsher —
at k = 128, per-position accuracy of 0.99 yields sequence accuracy of 0.28.

**Reproducibility limit — important.** The bf16 kernels use non-deterministic
atomics. On a cell that reliably solves, the same configuration and seed gives a
spread of ≈0.005 token accuracy. **On a bistable cell (§4.6) the same
configuration and the same seed has produced 0.1177 and 0.9598 — a spread of
0.84.** Single-run results on such cells carry no information and are labelled
accordingly below.

### 3.7 Methodological safeguards

Four rules were adopted during the project, three of them after an error that
would otherwise have entered a document.

1. **Every experiment file carries a self-test** against synthetic data whose
   answer is known in advance, and each test is verified to *fail* against the
   incorrect implementation. A test that has only ever passed demonstrates
   nothing.
2. **Controls come from the same invocation.** Comparisons against a number
   recorded by a different script, on a different day, are not admissible.
3. **On bistable cells, report the escape fraction, never a mean.** See §4.6.
4. **Attribution before mechanism.** Before attributing a failure to the
   published architecture, the experimental setup is ruled out first.

results reproduced on the 6 GB GPU. S₃ matches the published 1/0 pattern at

This matters because every comparison in this report is made against this
baseline. A broken baseline would invalidate everything downstream of it.

### 4.6 Trainability census (MEASURED — 50 runs; a methodological result)

Repeated runs of the **unmodified** architecture, 20 000 steps, constant learning
rate, data held fixed and only the run varied:

| cell | escapes / runs | rate | outcome shape |
|---|---|---|---|
| `A5_c5` @ n_h=2 | 4 / 14 | 0.29 | bimodal |
| `A5_c5_dt` @ n_h=2 | 2 / 10 | 0.20 | bimodal |
| `A5_c5_dt` @ n_h=4 | 1 / 10 | 0.10 | bimodal |
| `S5_c5_t` @ n_h=4 | 3 / 10 | 0.30 | bimodal |
| `S5_c5_t1` @ n_h=4 | — | — | continuous, 0.245 – 0.763 |

**Finding.** Outcomes on these cells are **bistable**: a run either escapes the
loss plateau and solves the task (≈0.99) or remains at chance (≈0.04), rarely
between. Escape is abrupt — loss falls two orders of magnitude within one logging
interval — and occurs anywhere from step 5 000 to step 20 000. Non-determinism in
the bf16 kernels alone selects the outcome; the same seed has produced both.

**Methodological consequence.** A single run of such a cell carries no
information. The correct summary statistic is the escape fraction over many runs.
Distinguishing a 25% escape rate from a 75% one at p < 0.05 with 80% power
requires approximately **20 runs per arm**.

**Consequence for this project's earlier position.** An earlier version of the
project record reported a "high-demand training wall" — that the architecture
could not train when most tokens require the maximum order, based on 0 of 4 cells
training. Under the measured escape rates, that observation occurs by chance
roughly 32% of the time, and a direct counterexample was subsequently obtained:
`S5_c5_t` at `n_h = 4` reached **token accuracy 0.9994, sequence accuracy
0.9415**, with parity accuracy 0.9995 on even targets and 0.9994 on odd. **The
wall claim is withdrawn.** The related interpretation that models stall at an
"A₅ ceiling" of 0.545 is likewise withdrawn: on the cell where the parity split
is measurable, the best run resolves *odd* targets at 0.7199, and the even/odd
gap narrows as runs improve.

**Why this matters more than it first appears.** Setting the measured escape
rates against the benefit adaptive order can deliver on each cell:

| notional benefit available | cells | trains reliably? |
|---|---|---|
| 2.29× – 3.48× | p = 0.05, 0.10, 0.25 | **yes**, ≥0.9997 |
| 1.28× | `S5_c5_t` | 3 / 10 |
| 1.03× | `S5_c5_t1` | no |
| 1.00× (nothing, by §3.3) | A₅ cells | 2–4 / 10 |

Every cell that trains reliably is one where adaptive order is worth 2.3–3.5×.
Every unreliable cell is one where it is worth 1.28× or less. **The instability
is confined to the regime in which the proposed mechanism has nothing to offer.**

### 4.8 The learned gate, end-to-end (FIRST EVIDENCE — 1 of 3 seeds)

Every adaptive figure in §4.5 came from an **oracle**: a control condition given
the correct per-token order from ground truth. This experiment is the first in
which the gate received no supervision on the allocation at all.

**Design.** `work/exp_gate_train.py`, p = 0.10. Three arms — `fixed4` (no gate),
`oracle` (gates from ground truth), `learned` (halting gate + budget penalty) —
at four budget weights γ ∈ {0, 0.01, 0.03, 0.1} and three random seeds: 18 runs,
all inside one invocation, so no comparison depends on a number from another
script. 20 000 steps each; the budget penalty is off for 4 000 steps and then
ramped over 4 000, so the gate cannot close before the task is learned. γ = 0 is
the control for the gate's mere presence. Gates are soft while training and
**hard at evaluation**, so every cost below is an integer count of Householder
factors.

**Result on the seed where the gate closed.**

| seed 1 | token acc | seq acc | cost/token | mae vs demand | corr | D=1 → | D=4 → |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| oracle | 0.9996 | 0.9770 | 1.298 | 0.00 | 1.00 | 1.000 | 4.000 |
| learned, γ = 0.03 | 0.9999 | 0.9910 | **1.298** | **0.00** | **1.00** | **1.000** | **4.000** |
| learned, γ = 0.1 | 1.0000 | 0.9970 | **1.298** | **0.00** | **1.00** | **1.000** | **4.000** |
| fixed4 | 0.9998 | 0.9885 | 4.000 | — | — | — | — |

The last two columns are the test that matters. A gate can reach the correct
*average* cost by closing uniformly on every token, which would be worthless, so
the script splits the spent order by the token's true demand and prints a warning
when that split is flat. Here it is not flat: every low-demand token received
exactly one factor and every high-demand token exactly four, across **256 000
evaluated tokens**, giving a mean absolute allocation error of zero to the
reported precision. Two independent budget weights produced the same allocation.

The accuracy differences against the oracle are within the run-to-run noise floor
established in §3.6 and are not claimed as an improvement over it.

**Result on the other two seeds.** Cost **exactly 4.000** at every γ including
0.1, with correlation 0.00 against demand — the gate never closed at all.

**What is bimodal is the gate, not the task.** Token accuracy is 0.98–1.00 on
all 18 runs, the non-closing ones included. This is a different phenomenon from
the accuracy bistability of §4.6.

**The γ = 0 control.** With the gate installed and never penalised, seed 1 still
fell to cost 3.369 with correlation 0.43, low-demand tokens averaging 3.30
against high-demand tokens at 4.00; seeds 2 and 3 stayed at 4.000. The task loss
alone had begun closing the gate on easy tokens, and the seed split is already
visible before any budget pressure is applied.

**Reported as a fraction, deliberately.** Outcomes on these cells are bistable
(§4.6), so the honest summary is *the gate closed on 1 of 3 seeds*, not a mean
cost over three runs — a mean of 1.298, 4.000 and 4.000 describes no run that
occurred.

**Limits.** This establishes neither a reliable mechanism nor a speedup. One
escaping seed in three is first evidence; the cost figure is required work per
token, not wall-clock, because Path A still computes all four factors and
multiplies the disabled ones by zero; and one operating point (p = 0.10) on one
task family is not coverage. The immediate next work item is making closure
reproducible, not writing this up.

### 4.9 Analysis — what the results collectively establish

The eight results above divide cleanly into three groups, and conflating them
would misrepresent the project.

**Proved, and not contingent on anything.** §4.2 and §4.3 are explicit
constructions in float64 with no optimiser involved. The two bounds and the
demand model hold regardless of whether any model ever trains. §4.4 is the
empirical prediction the demand model makes, and it separates perfectly on the
predicted variable, though at one run per cell.

**Measured with an oracle, i.e. an upper bound on the mechanism.** §4.5 shows
that *if* the per-token order were known, 2.29×–3.48× less required work suffices
for full accuracy where the cheapest fixed order pays 4. This bounds what a
learned gate could achieve and says nothing about whether one can.

**Measured with a learned gate, once.** §4.8 closes part of that gap — the gate
reached the oracle allocation exactly, with zero allocation error — but on 1 seed
in 3. The honest reading is that the mechanism is *possible* and not yet
*reliable*.

Two cross-cutting observations tie these together. First, §4.6 shows the
instability of the base architecture is confined to configurations where adaptive
order would deliver 1.28× or less, while every configuration worth 2.3× or more
trains reliably — so the architecture's weakest regime and the mechanism's useful
regime do not overlap. Second, §4.8 shows the *gate's* failure is independent of
task accuracy, which was 0.98–1.00 everywhere: the two bistabilities are
different phenomena and must not be pooled.

The gap between §4.5 and §4.8 — between what a perfect gate achieves and what a
trained one achieves two times in six — is the quantity the next phase is
measured against.

## 5. Conclusion and Future Work

### 5.1 Conclusion

**What is established.** The project has produced a correction to the
expressivity cost model assumed in this line of work, proved to machine
precision: the number of Householder factors a token requires is determined by
the closure of the entire input alphabet, not by the token. The immediate
practical consequence is that `n_h` is a hyperparameter a practitioner cannot
choose correctly, since doing so requires knowing the minimum faithful
representation dimension of the generated group. A layer that learns its own
order removes a knob that cannot be set by hand — an argument that holds today
and requires no speedup to hold.

**What has first evidence but is not established.** The monotone halting gate
exists, passes 7/7 unit tests including gradient flow, satisfies the parity
constraint §3.2 shows is necessary, and has now been trained end-to-end once. On
1 of 3 seeds it recovered the oracle's per-token allocation exactly — zero mean
absolute error over 256 000 tokens, with no supervision on the allocation. On the
other 2 seeds it never closed. One escaping seed in three is not a working
mechanism, and every other adaptive figure in this report still comes from an
oracle supplied with ground truth.

**What has not been demonstrated.** No wall-clock speedup exists. Path A computes
all factors and multiplies unused ones by zero; the adaptive and full-cost arms
differ by 0.7%, within noise. Realising the measured 2.3–3.5× reduction in
required work as actual time requires ragged expansion, which is not built.

**A methodological result.** Outcomes on the harder cells are bistable, and
single-run measurements of them are uninformative. Applying this correctly
withdrew two claims from the project record — a purported training wall and an
associated capability ceiling — and established that the architecture's
instability is confined to the regime where the proposed mechanism offers no
benefit. The regime the project depends on trains reliably.

**Position entering the second phase.** The theoretical contribution is secure.
The engineering contribution has started and is not yet reliable. The question
has moved from *can a gate learn the allocation* — it can, demonstrably, at least
sometimes — to *can it be made to do so consistently*, which is a narrower and
more tractable question than the one the project held a week ago.

### 5.2 Future work

**Immediate — make gate closure reproducible (O4 continued).** The failure mode
on the non-closing seeds looks like saturation at initialisation rather than
insufficient budget pressure: γ = 0.1 left those runs at cost *exactly* 4.000
rather than partway down, the gate's output weight is initialised to exactly
zero — making it input-independent until that weight moves — and its output bias
of 4.0 places the sigmoid at 0.982, where the derivative is 0.018. Two targeted
interventions are implemented as flags and will be tested one at a time, over ten
seeds each: a lower initialisation bias, and a separate learning rate for the
gate.

**Then — the sharpened form of the demand model.** Does a learned gate discover
that a 5-cycle costs 2 under an A₅ alphabet but 4 under an S₅ one? That would be
the gate inferring a global algebraic property from purely local statistics, a
substantially better interpretability result than "the gate correlates with
difficulty". This is blocked on the previous item: a mechanism that fires one
time in three cannot support it.

**Then — Path B and the efficiency claim (O5).** Ragged expansion, benchmarked at
batch 1 and batch 128. Until this exists, no speedup may be claimed anywhere.

**Then — breadth (O6).** Extend the demand model empirically to S₃, S₄ and A₄ to
confirm D predicts required order across groups rather than within S₅ only; and
validate on board-state tracking, a task this project did not design.

**Outstanding smaller items.** The group-closure grid needs re-running with
multiple runs per cell; the broader literature survey (§2.2) is not done; and
length extrapolation for the `fixed3` anomaly at p ≤ 0.25 remains untested.

### 5.3 Decision points and pre-registered fallbacks

The plan has pre-registered fallbacks rather than an assumption of success. If
**O4** fails — meaning the gate reaches the oracle allocation only occasionally
and no intervention raises that rate — the outcome is a theory contribution with
an oracle control, not a mechanism paper. Note that the failure condition has
already narrowed: §4.8 shows the gate *can* recover the allocation exactly, so
what is now at stake is reliability rather than possibility. If **O5** shows
ragged expansion is not faster in practice, the efficiency argument is dropped
and the contribution rests on removing an unguessable hyperparameter, which
§3.3 establishes independently of any speedup. Both fallbacks remain genuine
contributions; neither is to be presented as the original plan.
